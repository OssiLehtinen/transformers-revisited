Mixture of experts:
hire more, consult few

the parameters balloon; the meter barely moves

This door opens off section 10, and it pulls on the lopsided fact reported there: the MLPs hold roughly two-thirds of a transformer's parameters — they are where the remembering lives. Behind this door, that concentration becomes a lever. You'll want section 10's picture of the MLP as questions-then-votes, section 17's picture of the model as a wide bank of workers on one bus, and — when the bill arrives — the corridor's running currency exchange between arithmetic and bytes, quoted behind door 01. The trick itself fits in a sentence: keep many MLPs where the blueprint shows one, and send each token to only a couple of them. The rest of the door is why that works, what it costs, and what the word "expert" is doing in the name.

01Memories that mostly sit idle

Everything we know about scaling says the same thing: more parameters, trained on more tokens, make a better model. And section 10 told us where the parameters pool — in the MLPs, the banks of key–value memories, the questions and the votes. So the obvious way to a better model is fatter MLPs, and it works, and it has an equally obvious problem: in the dense transformer we built, every parameter is exercised for every token. Each position's vector is scored against every feature detector in every layer, at training time and inference time alike. Double the memories, double the arithmetic — the two costs are welded together.

But watch those memories at work and the welding starts to look silly. The detector for a Python def statement's rhythm contributes nothing to a French cooking blog. The features that matter for legal boilerplate sit idle through a physics derivation. Whatever a token needs from the MLP, it plainly doesn't need all of it — the questions worth asking of any one token are a sliver of the questions the model knows how to ask. The nonlinearity already says as much: most detectors, most of the time, fire at zero and contribute nothing but the arithmetic it took to check. A big model's knowledge is consulted sparsely. Its cost, in the dense design, is paid densely.

So the ambition writes itself: separate the two meters. Grow the stock of memories without growing the per-token bill. What's needed is a mechanism that looks at a token and decides which sliver of the memory bank is worth waking up — and we happen to own a differentiable device for exactly that decision.

02A panel of experts and a tiny router

Here is the whole mechanism. Where the blueprint shows one MLP per layer, install E of them — a panel of so-called experts, each a full questions-and-votes MLP of its own. In front of the panel, add a router: a single tiny matrix whose E rows are probes, one per expert. A token's vector arrives from the stream; the router dots it against each probe — the same match-scoring that has powered every search in this essay — softmaxes the scores into preferences, and keeps only the top k, with k as small as 1 or 2. Only those experts run. Their outputs are blended, weighted by the router's preferences, and written back to the stream:

One layer's MoE block. The router is a four-row lookup table; the search
decides not where to look (attention's job) but which memories to wake.

Note what kind of object the router is: a dot-product search over learned probes, run per token, per layer — attention's core move, pointed at a new question. Attention asks which positions matter; the router asks which specialists do. And because the blend weights come out of a softmax, the choice is (mostly) trainable: gradients flow through the weights gi to the router's probes, teaching it to send tokens where they're best served. There's an honesty note hiding in that "mostly" — engineering reality #2 demands differentiability, and the hard top-k cut is not differentiable. No gradient ever tells expert 2 what it would have done with the token it didn't get. Training therefore only refines choices among experts that get sampled into use — which is why the whole scheme needs the babysitting described in section 04, and why the elegant summary "it's just a learned dispatch" has a small asterisk welded to it.

03The accounting: two meters, finally separate

Now read the meters. With E experts and top-k routing, the layer's stock of parameters — its memories — grows by a factor of E. The per-token arithmetic grows only by k (the router itself is a rounding error: one E-row matmul against MLPs thousands of times larger). The two costs that the dense design welded together now move independently, and real models put a wide gap between them:

                          total params    active per token
Mixtral 8×7B  (top-2 of 8)      47 B            13 B
DeepSeek-V3  (top-8 of 256,     671 B           37 B
              + 1 shared expert)
The two meters, in the wild. "Active" is what one token's forward pass
touches; the rest of the parameters sit in memory, waiting for a token
that concerns them.

This is what "sparse" means in the phrase sparse model: not sparse matrices, but conditional computation — a vast model of which each token runs a narrow slice. The slice is chosen afresh at every layer, so across 60 layers a token threads its own path through the panels, and two tokens in the same sentence may share almost none of it.

Spot the corridor's currency exchange, running in reverse. Doors 01 and 02 spent cheap arithmetic to avoid expensive bytes. MoE spends bytes to avoid arithmetic: all 671 billion parameters must sit resident in (expensive, distributed) memory so that any token can wake any expert, but each token's compute — the thing that scales with every single one of the trillions of tokens in a training run — stays modest. At training scale that trade is overwhelmingly right, which is why, behind various curtains of corporate discretion, essentially every frontier lab now ships models that are sparse in this sense. The sign on this door said most frontier models live behind it; they do.

04Keeping the panel honest

Left to itself, the elegant mechanism falls over, and it falls in an instructive direction. Early in training, some expert is — by pure initialization luck — slightly better than its peers at the average token. The router, doing its job, sends it slightly more traffic. More traffic means more gradient, means it improves faster, means the router likes it even more. The feedback loop runs away, and you end with one exhausted generalist doing all the work while the rest of the panel, never chosen and therefore never taught (remember the asterisk from section 02: no gradient reaches the unchosen), atrophies into expensive decoration. Router collapse is the failure mode; rich-get-richer is its mechanism.

The standard cure is refreshingly unsubtle: add a small load-balancing term to the loss that rewards spreading traffic evenly across the panel, and let it argue with the router's preferences. Tuning that argument is a genuine tension — balance perfectly and you've abolished specialization, the entire point; balance too weakly and you collapse — and the engineering around it (capacity limits per expert, with overflow tokens skipping the MLP entirely via the residual path in early systems; auxiliary-loss-free schemes that instead quietly nudge a per-expert bias, in recent ones) is a large share of what separates MoE recipes that train from MoE recipes that make headlines about instability. None of it changes the mental model; all of it decides whether the mental model survives contact with gradient descent.

05What "expert" doesn't mean

The name invites a picture: a French expert, a code expert, a chemistry expert, each presiding over a topic. The honesty note is that when researchers went looking for that picture in trained routers, they mostly didn't find it. Routing correlates with shallow, token-level structure — punctuation, indentation, common function words, syntactic position — far more than with human subject matter, and a given expert's caseload reads less like a specialty and more like a bureaucrat's inbox: coherent to the filing system, illegible to visitors. The router's categories are its own, discovered because they made the loss go down, and nothing asked them to align with a library's. (After section 17's superposition caveat, this should feel familiar: the model's internal taxonomy rarely matches ours. The recent trend even leans into it — many small experts, finely sliced, with a shared expert always in the blend to hold the load-bearing generalities — a design that has given up entirely on experts being legible and just wants the sparsity.)

The residual-stream picture from section 17 absorbs all of this without strain — and it's the best way to file MoE mentally. The model was already a wide bank of workers wired to one bus, most of them effectively idle for any given token (that's what a detector firing at zero is). MoE makes the idleness architectural: the bank of workers per stop gets enormously wider, an explicit dispatcher chooses the few who clock in, and the unchosen cost nothing instead of costing a wasted check. The crowd got bigger; the meeting stayed small. Why do this to the MLPs and not to attention? Because the lever only pays where the parameters pool — and that fact, from section 10, is the one this whole door stands on.

06The economics, and the next door

The serving story has the same two-sided character as everything else in this corridor. For a provider running huge batches across racks of accelerators, MoE is a gift: the experts are sharded across devices (expert parallelism), every device stays busy because a big enough batch contains tokens for everyone, and each user's token enjoys the quality of a 671B-parameter model at the arithmetic price of a 37B one. For a single user on a single machine, the same design is a tax: all the parameters must fit in local memory — door 01's scarcest resource — while the compute they enable goes mostly unused at batch size one. Sparse models are, by construction, models that pool best. It's the rare architectural choice whose goodness depends on how many of you there are.

And with that, the machine's blueprint is as scaled-up as this corridor takes it: cached, tiled, stretched, and now sparse — every door so far about making the same next-token predictor bigger, longer, or cheaper. The remaining question is different in kind: how a text-continuation machine becomes something you can talk to. That's post-training — instruction tuning and RLHF — and its door, the next one down, is still marked with a sign.
