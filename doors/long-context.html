Long context:
stretching the clock

how a model trained on 8,000 tokens reads a novel

This door opens off section 13, and that section is a hard prerequisite: everything here happens to RoPE's rotating probes, the clock hands with their fast and slow frequencies. The first two doors — the KV cache and FlashAttention — are about the cost of a long context, and they'll be referenced when the bill shows up. But cost is the smaller half of this story. The stranger half is that a model trained on sequences of 8,000 tokens doesn't merely slow down past 8,000 — it falls apart, even though nothing in the attention equation mentions a maximum length at all. Behind this door: why it breaks, the clock-repair tricks that fix it, and an honest look at whether the model that can read a novel actually does.

01Two walls, not one

Nothing in the machinery we built has a hard length limit. Attention compares every query against every key, however many there are; the MLPs work position by position and couldn't care less. So why does a context window have a number on it?

Because there are two separate walls. The first is the bill: attention's arithmetic grows quadratically with length (door 02 made it cheaper to pay, not smaller), and the KV cache charges rent per token held in memory (door 01's entire ledger). The second wall is sneakier: generalization. A model is a creature of its training distribution, and a model trained on 8k-token documents has simply never seen what position 30,000 looks like. Asked to continue a longer sequence anyway, it doesn't degrade politely — perplexity doesn't creep up, it detonates, often within a few hundred tokens past the boundary.

The second wall is the interesting one, because to locate it precisely you have to ask: seen what, exactly? The token embeddings don't know about position. The residual stream doesn't. With RoPE, position lives in exactly one place — the angles of the rotated search probes. So that is where long context has to break, and where it has to be fixed.

02What actually breaks: angles nobody swept

Recall the clock from section 13. Each pair of dimensions in a query or key is a hand that winds forward as position advances, and the hands run at wildly different speeds. With the standard settings, the fastest hand completes a full turn every ~6 tokens; the slowest needs about 63,000 tokens to come around once. That spread was the design: second hands to separate neighbors, hour hands to separate chapters.

Now train on 8k-token documents and take inventory of what each hand experienced:

fig 1 · the training inventory of three clock hands. highlighted arcs are angles actually visited during training; the dashed hand asks for one that wasn't.

The fast hands wrapped around thousands of times — every angle on their dials appeared in training, so no position will ever surprise them. But the slowest hand, with its 63k-token period, crawled through a mere 46° of its dial. The model's learned probes — every "match this, but prefer recent instances" behavior it acquired — were fit to key-query angle combinations drawn from that thin arc. Position 30,000 puts the slow hands somewhere they have literally never pointed. The dot products come back with values outside anything the softmax's learned temperature expects; scores that should be gentle preferences become lurching noise; and the next-token distribution goes to pieces. The model didn't learn "position" in the abstract. It learned behavior on the angles it saw.

Framed that way, the fix suggests itself. We can't quickly teach the model new angles — that's what expensive long-context training is. But we control the clock. The angles are computed from the position index at inference time; nothing stops us from lying about the time.

03First fix: slow the whole clock down

Position interpolation is the blunt version of the lie. To run a model trained at 8k out to 32k, divide every position index by 4 before computing the rotations. Token 32,000 now claims to sit at position 8,000; every hand stays within the arc it swept during training; nothing is ever out of distribution.

real positions:      0 ……………………………… 32,000
                      ↓        divide by 4        ↓
angles fed to RoPE:  0 ………… 8,000   ← the swept arc, reused
Interpolation: compress the novel into the stretch of dial the model knows.
Nothing is extrapolated; everything is crowded.

It works — with a short fine-tune to let the model acclimatize, a few thousand steps rather than a retraining. But listen to what it does to the fast hands. The second hand used to advance a quarter-turn per token; now it advances a sixteenth. Adjacent tokens, which the model told apart by crisp, well-separated angles, now huddle four times closer together on every dial. Local resolution — exactly the thing the fast hands existed to provide — gets blurred, and it shows up as a real (if modest) hit on ordinary short-context tasks. We rewound the hour hand, which was necessary, and also the second hand, which was vandalism.

04Second fix: repair each hand separately

The obvious refinement: the hands are independent, so lie to each one differently. The fast hands have wrapped many times — every angle on their dial is familiar territory — so leave them running at full speed and full resolution. The slow hands are the ones about to point into the void, so slow them down until 32,000 tokens fits inside their swept arc. Hands in between get scaled in between.

This is the shared idea behind the NTK-aware scalings and YaRN, which differ in bookkeeping rather than in spirit: per-frequency interpolation, judged by how many times each hand wrapped during training. (YaRN adds one more repair that answers for a different symptom: with vastly more tokens in view, each softmax row spreads its attention thinner — more candidates, more entropy — so it applies a small temperature correction to the scores, resharpening the search. Two fixes, two failure modes, one method.) The result was startlingly effective: extensions from 8k or 32k out to 128k with brief fine-tuning became the standard recipe, and variants of it sit inside most of the long-context models you have actually used.

Two closing notes on the clock itself. First, models trained since this all became understood mostly bake the lesson in from the start: crank the base of the frequency spectrum up (the rope_theta you'll meet in model configs, 10,000 in the original recipe, 500,000 or more now), so even the slow hands turn slowly enough that enormous contexts stay inside a modest arc, then include genuinely long documents late in training. Retrofit surgery became factory spec. Second — honesty — none of this made position 200,000 meaningful. It made the angles at position 200,000 familiar. Whether the model can do anything useful with a probe at that distance is a question about training data and attention capacity, and it's the subject of the last section. A stretched clock is not the same thing as a long memory.

05The other wall: not attending to everything

Meanwhile the first wall still stands. A 128k context means every new token's probe scores 128k keys (door 02 makes this fast, not free), and door 01's ledger says the cache for it occupies gigabytes per sequence, read end-to-end for every generated token. The second family of long-context tricks accepts a blunt bargain: don't attend to everything.

The workhorse is sliding-window attention: each position attends only to the last w tokens — the causal mask's triangle trimmed to a diagonal band. This looks like giving up on long range entirely, but stack the layers and watch the information travel:

layer 3:                        ▓ ← sees t−3w … t, via two hops
layer 2:              ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
layer 1:    ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
tokens:   …──────────────────────t
A window of w per layer, but the residual stream carries what layer 1 gathered
into layer 2's window: reach compounds by ~w per layer, like receptive fields in a CNN.

Each token's slot in the residual stream summarizes its neighborhood; the next layer's window reads those summaries, so effective reach grows by a window per layer — distant content arrives in hops rather than one glance. And the cache rent stops growing: a windowed layer keeps only w entries, period (the corridor's doors keep opening onto each other). Production models mostly mix the two kinds of layer — a few full-attention layers interleaved among many windowed ones, on the theory that most retrieval is local and a handful of layers with a telescope can serve the rest.

One genuinely odd footnote from this line of work: models lean surprisingly hard on the first few tokens of the sequence — evict them from a sliding cache and generation destabilizes. The leading explanation is that softmax must distribute weight somewhere even when a probe matches nothing, and the ever-present earliest tokens become the designated dumping ground — an attention sink. Long-context serving systems now simply pin those tokens in the cache. File under: the machine develops habits its blueprint never mentions.

06Does it actually read the novel?

So the clock is stretched, the window is managed, the rent is paid, and the spec sheet says 1,000,000 tokens. The honesty section asks: does the model use them?

The standard first check is a needle in a haystack: bury one odd fact in a vast context and ask for it. Stretched models mostly pass — the searchlight, once the angles are familiar, is genuinely good at finding a distinctive match at range, which is unsurprising for a machine whose core primitive is a batch of dot-product searches. But passing it is table stakes, not reading. Ask instead for something that requires holding the whole text in mind — tallying every appearance of a character, combining a clause on page 12 with its amendment on page 840 — and performance degrades well before the advertised limit, with a famous bias pattern: strongest near the beginning and end of the context, weakest in the middle. The effective context of a model is routinely shorter than its accepted context, and the gap between the two numbers is precisely what the marketing does not print.

It's also worth saying what long context is for, because there's a rival with the same job description. If the task is "answer questions against a big pile of documents," you can attend over the pile — or you can retrieve from it: embed the documents, embed the question, and fetch the best dot-product matches into a short, dense context (retrieval-augmented generation, RAG). Notice what that is: the search primitive from section 02, run outside the model, over a corpus instead of a residual stream. The field's actual practice settled into using both — retrieval to select what deserves attention, long context to let the model see all of what was selected at once.

The moral of the door, then, in one line each. Length is two problems, cost and familiarity, with separate cures. Position is angles, and angles can be repaired at the clock face. And a context window is exactly that — glass you can see through, not memory you can rely on; how the model's real memory works when the window closes is a story that runs through several of the doors still marked in the corridor.
