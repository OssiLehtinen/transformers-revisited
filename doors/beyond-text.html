Beyond text,
and beyond attention

the stream doesn't know it's reading

The last door in the corridor opens off section 12, on the strength of one sentence there: after the embedding, everything happens to dense vectors — the one-hot symbols appear only at the entrance and the exit. That makes the entrance a border crossing, and borders can be crossed by anything with papers. The first half of this door is how images and audio get their papers — the trick Rohrer's original essay anticipated in its audio example, now the standard architecture of every model that looks at your screenshots. The second half turns around and walks the other way: attention itself, the mechanism this whole essay built toward, has rivals — recurrence, reformed and returned — and the honest question is how much of the job they can take back. Both halves end at the same place: section 17's residual stream, unbothered.

01The entrance is the only text-specific part

Inventory the machine with border control in mind. The tokenizer (section 21) is about text. The embedding table is indexed by tokens of text. And that's the end of the list: from the first residual stream vector onward, nothing downstream — not the attention searches, not the MLP votes, not the normalization — knows or cares that the [n × d] matrix flowing through it began as words. The stream is a bus for dense vectors of width d, full stop. Section 12 called the embedding a projection from symbol space into a shared geometric space; the load-bearing word was shared.

So the recipe for feeding the machine something that isn't text writes itself: don't touch the machine. Build a new entrance — some way of turning your signal into a sequence of d-dimensional vectors — and merge onto the stream. Everything the corridor established (the cache, the tiling, the stretched clocks, the expert panels, the post-training) applies instantly to whatever comes through, because none of it ever mentioned words either.

02Images: a sentence of patches

For images, the entrance is almost insultingly simple. Slice the image into a grid of small square patches — 16 × 16 pixels is traditional — flatten each patch's pixel values into one long vector, and push each through a single learned linear projection (section 12's move, minus the one-hot: a lookup table softened into a linear map) to width d. Read the patches left-to-right, top-to-bottom, like text:

  ┌────┬────┬────┬────┐
  │ p1 │ p2 │ p3 │ p4 │     one 16×16 patch × 3 color channels = 768 raw numbers
  ├────┼────┼────┼────┤         │ learned linear projection
  │ p5 │ p6 │ …       │         ▼
  └────┴────┴────┴────┘     one d-dimensional "visual token"

  image  →  [ p1 p2 p3 p4 p5 p6 … ]  →  the same transformer
The vision transformer's entrance: an image becomes a sequence of patch
vectors — a sentence whose words are little squares.

That's the vision transformer (ViT), and the shock of it in 2020 was everything it declined to include. Computer vision's decades of built-in visual wisdom — convolutions, locality, translation invariance — replaced by: make it a sequence, add a position signal (section 13's chapter, again — in two dimensions now: learned 2D embeddings classically, and increasingly 2D flavors of RoPE), and let attention learn which patches should search for which. Given enough data, the general machine matched and then passed the specialized ones — the bitterest of lessons, delivered one more time, this time to pixels.

A chat model that reads your screenshots is then a plumbing diagram: a vision encoder turns the image into a few hundred visual tokens; a small learned adapter projects them into the language model's d and dialect; and they're spliced into the conversation sequence between the text tokens, where the language model attends over them exactly as it attends over words. When you ask "what's wrong with this error message?", the model's probes are running the same dot-product searches over patch vectors and word vectors alike — one stream, mixed citizenship, no separate visual brain.

03Audio, and everything else with a sequence in it

Audio was the modality Rohrer's original essay used to introduce the whole subject, and his framing — signal in, sampled and vectorized, sequence out — was this door's idea waiting for its moment. The modern entrance: chop the waveform into short frames, summarize each as a spectral vector (which frequencies, how loudly — a small honest bit of classical signal processing), or increasingly, train a neural codec that compresses audio into a vocabulary of discrete tokens, BPE's spiritual cousin (section 21) for sound. Either way: a sequence of vectors, the border crossed, done. Speech recognition, once a cathedral of specialist components — acoustic models, pronunciation dictionaries, language models negotiating over lattices — collapsed into "transcribe" as one long sequence continuation.

And the pattern generalizes past the classic modalities: protein sequences, robot action streams, weather grids, mouse-and-keyboard events — the field's default first move for any new domain is now "find the tokens, then bring the transformer." Two honesty notes keep the triumph in proportion. Tokenizing a continuous world is lossy in ways worth remembering — section 21 warned that the model never sees the letters, and a patch grid likewise never quite sees the pixel. And generating in continuous modalities is genuinely split: models that produce images and video as of this writing often pair a transformer backbone with a diffusion process — start from noise, iteratively denoise — a different generative recipe that earns a corridor of its own somewhere else. The universal reader is settled fact; the universal writer is still being argued about.

04Beyond attention: recurrence, reformed

Now the other direction. This essay's founding villain was recurrence — the 2017 paper's title was a shot at it, and section 16 told the story: attention won because every position could be trained in parallel, while recurrent networks marched token by token. But sit with the corridor's cost ledger — quadratic arithmetic (door 02 made it fast, not small), a KV cache that grows without bound and is re-read for every token (door 01's rent) — and the old idea starts to whisper: a recurrent model carries a fixed-size state. Constant memory. Linear time. No cache rent at all. What if recurrence could be built to train in parallel, the one virtue it lacked?

That's the modern state-space model family — Mamba is the name that stuck — and one honest cartoon captures both its promise and its price:

attention:  a growing archive          recurrence:  a fixed-size notebook
  every past token kept, verbatim;       the past compressed into one
  any probe may search all of it,        state vector, updated per token;
  any time.                              what wasn't written down is gone.

  cost: rent forever (door 01)           cost: forgetting, forever
The trade at the heart of the rivalry. Attention pays memory for perfect
recall; recurrence pays recall for constant memory.

The reformation that made the notebook competitive is selectivity: the state update is gated by the input, so the model chooses, token by token, what to write into its limited notebook and what to let fade — a learned, differentiable act of triage (engineering reality #2, still on duty). With some serious engineering to keep training parallel, these models genuinely deliver: linear-time processing, constant-memory generation, no quadratic wall, no cache ledger. On many tasks they match attention outright. Where they crack is exactly where the cartoon says they must: verbatim recall at range — door 03's needle tests, retrieving a phone number from two hundred pages back — because a fixed-size summary, however cleverly triaged, eventually declines to contain everything. Attention's defining extravagance, keeping every token forever, turns out to be the feature hardest to give up.

05The armistice: hybrids

So the field, with uncharacteristic good sense, stopped treating it as a duel. If attention layers are archives and state-space layers are notebooks, a model can staff itself with mostly notebooks and a few archivists: interleave many cheap linear-time layers with a handful of full-attention layers that provide exact recall when a probe truly needs the verbatim past. If that rhymes, it should — it's door 03's sliding-window-plus-global-layers mix again, with the local half swapped for something smarter than a window, and the corridor's doors opening onto each other one last time. Hybrids along these lines have shipped in production model families, and the frontier's drift is visible in every architecture note: the expensive, exact, quadratic mechanism reserved as a minority ingredient; the cheap sequence-mixer doing the bulk hauling. How far that ratio can fall — whether attention ends as the salt rather than the meal — is a live question the next revision of this page will answer differently than this one.

06The stream survives; the corridor closes

Look at what this door did to the essay's machine, because it's the corridor's whole method in miniature. We swapped the entrance (patches and codecs for tokenizers), and the stream didn't notice. The rivals swap the sequence-mixer (notebooks for archives), and the stream doesn't notice that either: a state-space layer, exactly like an attention head, exactly like an MLP, is a worker that reads the residual stream, computes, and writes back by addition. Section 17's picture — one wide additive bus, a crowd of workers, a thin discipline of read/compute/write — is the invariant every renovation in this corridor preserved, and the safest bet in a field with few safe bets is that whatever architecture headlines the next decade will preserve it too. The residual stream is less one design choice among many than the discovery of a good way for learned components to share a workspace; the components, this door suggests, are negotiable.

And that closes the corridor — six doors, all open. The essay built one machine out of counting and dot products; the doors watched that machine get cached, tiled, stretched, sparsified, civilized, and finally generalized until neither its input nor its centerpiece was sacred. If a seventh door ever appears in section 22 — the diffusion corridor is right there, unexcavated — it will follow the same house rules: no black boxes, honesty notes attached, contributions additive.
