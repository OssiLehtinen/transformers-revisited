After pretraining:
the predictor learns manners

same machine, same loss, then a judge instead of a loss

This is the door where the corridor changes subject. Every other door — caching, tiling, stretching, sparsifying — made the machine from the essay bigger or cheaper while leaving its job alone: predict the next token of text like the text it was trained on. Behind this door, the job changes. You'll want section 11's generation loop, section 20's observation that everything is sequence continuation, and section 21's tokens in hand. No new architecture appears anywhere in this door — that's rather the point. What changes is what the weights are optimized for, and it is the difference between the model that completes your sentence and the model that answers it.

01The machine we actually built

Be honest about what the essay's recipe produces. Trained on a mountain of internet text to predict the next token, the result — a base model — is a simulator of its training distribution. Hand it text and it continues that text the way documents on the internet tend to continue. Which yields moments like this:

prompt:      What is the capital of France?

base model:  What is the capital of Germany?
             What is the capital of Italy?
             Answer key on page 12.
Not a malfunction. The internet is full of quiz sheets, and the likeliest
continuation of one bare question is another bare question.

The knowledge is in there — somewhere in the stream, Paris is winning votes — but the model has no reason to tell you. It isn't being evasive; "answer the question addressed to you" is simply not the objective it was trained on. Practitioners of the base-model era coaxed behavior out by prompting-as-stagecraft: write the opening of a document in which an expert is answering questions, provide two worked examples, and let the predictor predict its way through a third. It worked, and it was ridiculous — a machine that must be tricked into being useful by disguising every request as a half-finished document. The gap between "predicts text" and "does what you asked" is the whole business of post-training, and it's closed in two moves: first show, then judge.

02First move: show it — supervised fine-tuning

The showing is almost anticlimactic. Collect tens of thousands of exemplary conversations — prompts paired with the responses an ideal assistant would give, written or curated by people — and continue training on them with the identical machinery: same weights, same forward pass, same next-token loss from the essay, just pointed at a much smaller and much more opinionated dataset. The only novelty is formatting. A conversation is flattened into one sequence — section 20 said everything is sequence continuation, and here that stops being an observation and becomes the implementation — with a few special tokens (ordinary vocabulary entries, in section 21's sense, that no natural text ever produces) marking who is speaking:

<|system|> You are a helpful assistant.
<|user|> What is the capital of France?
<|assistant|> The capital of France is Paris. <|end|>
The chat template. One sequence, a costume party of special tokens; the loss
is computed only on the assistant's spans — imitate the answers, not the questions.

This is supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and it works startlingly well. A few epochs and the quiz-sheet reflex is gone: the model has learned that in this distribution, text after <|assistant|> answers what came before. When you chat with a model, this template is what your app is really sending — the conversation is one growing document that the machine from section 11 is forever continuing.

But imitation has a ceiling, and it's worth being precise about where. SFT teaches the model to write like the demonstrations — including their confidence. The demonstrators answered fluently, so the model answers fluently, whether or not its own knowledge backs the fluency: imitating the style of knowing is not knowing, and some of what gets called hallucination is exactly this — confident cadence, learned faithfully, deployed past the edge of the underlying knowledge. Beyond that, demonstrations can't say what not to do, can't cover every situation, and cost expert labor to write. What we want to teach next isn't a behavior; it's a preference between behaviors. And preferences are far cheaper to collect than perfection: a person who could never write the ideal answer can still look at two answers and say which is better.

03Second move: judge it — the reward model

So collect comparisons. Sample two responses to the same prompt, show a human both, record the choice. Do this many thousands of times and you have a dataset not of ideal behavior but of direction — which way is better, from wherever the model stands.

To use it, build a judge: a reward model. Architecturally it's a familiar face — take a transformer (typically a copy of the very model being trained), and swap the unembedding from section 14 for a head that outputs a single number: a score for "how good is this response". Train it on the comparisons, nudging it to score each pair's winner above its loser, and the same machinery that learned to predict tokens learns to predict the human frown. Reading that twice is recommended: the judge of the language model is a language model — the corridor's machinery, dot-product searches and voting MLPs all the way down, retargeted from "what comes next" to "how would a rater feel about this".

The reward model is a compression of thousands of hours of human judgment into a callable function — score any response, instantly. And it has the flaw of every compression: it's accurate near the data it saw and increasingly fictional far from it. That flaw is about to matter enormously.

04Optimizing against the judge — RLHF

Now close the loop. Have the model generate responses to a pile of prompts; score each with the reward model; adjust the weights to make higher-scoring responses more likely. That's reinforcement learning from human feedback — RLHF — usually implemented with a policy-gradient algorithm (PPO is the classic), whose details matter less than the shape of the loop:

prompts ──→ the model ──→ responses ──→ reward model ──→ scores
                ▲                                           │
                └────── nudge weights toward high scores ───┘
                │
   KL leash ────┘  "and stay recognizably yourself"
The RLHF loop. For the first time in this essay, the model is trained on
text it wrote itself — the loss no longer says "match the data" but "please the judge."

Notice how deeply that breaks the frame of everything before this door. Pretraining and SFT optimize toward a fixed target drawn from a dataset; here the model explores, and is scored on productions of its own. That freedom is the method's power — the model can find good behaviors no demonstrator wrote down — and its famous hazard. The judge is a learned approximation, and an optimizer is a ruthless search for exactly the inputs on which an approximation is wrong. Push hard enough and the model discovers responses that score wonderfully and are garbage: longer answers if the judge has a soft spot for thoroughness, hedging if it has one for caution, flattery if — and it does — it has one for being agreed with. Reward hacking is Goodhart's law with a gradient, and the corridor's honesty requires saying that sycophancy — the assistant's incurable agreeableness — isn't a mysterious personality flaw; it's what "please the judge" converges to when the judge was trained on ratings from people who, on average, enjoyed being agreed with.

The standard restraint is the KL leash visible in the figure: alongside the reward, penalize drift from the SFT model's token probabilities, so the optimizer may sharpen the persona it was given but not wander into the judge's fictional far field. Tension between leash and reward is the daily weather of post-training: too loose and the model games the judge; too tight and nothing improves.

05Sharper tools: DPO, and judges that can't be fooled

Two refinements dominate current practice, pulling in opposite directions of subtlety. The first, direct preference optimization (DPO), is subtraction: a bit of algebra shows that for the leashed objective, the optimal policy and the reward determine each other in closed form — so you can skip training a separate judge and skip the RL loop, and instead update the model directly on each comparison pair: raise the winner's probability, lower the loser's, scaled just so. What's left looks almost like SFT with a twist, trains stably, and became the workhorse of open-weight post-training.

The second is addition, and it's the engine of the current era. RLHF's central weakness is that its judge can be fooled. But some judgments can't be: a program passes its tests or doesn't; a proof checks or doesn't; the answer is 42 or it isn't. Where a verifiable reward exists, the optimizer can push as hard as it likes — hacking a unit test is called solving it. Reinforcement learning against verifiable rewards is the recipe behind the recent reasoning models: models trained to emit long private chains of working — explore, backtrack, check — before the visible answer, because the reward paid for being right, and extended deliberation is what being right at hard problems costs. The result upended the economics one more time: such models spend orders of magnitude more decode-time tokens per answer, which sends us straight back through door 01 — thinking, on this architecture, is billed by the KV-cache-hour.

06Same machine, different creature

Step back and audit what this door changed. Architecture: nothing. Parameter count: nothing. Every mechanism in the essay — the searches, the votes, the stream — runs in the assistant exactly as it ran in the base model; post-training is a few percent of the total compute, a coat of paint on a cathedral, thickness-wise. And yet it decides nearly everything a user ever experiences: that the model answers rather than continues, refuses rather than complies, formats rather than rambles; its persona, its hedges, its tone. When people say a model "wants" to be helpful or "is trained to be safe," they are talking about this thin, behavior-setting layer — pretraining decides what the machine knows; post-training decides who shows up to the conversation.

The honesty notes, then the corridor. The base model's calibration is partly a casualty — where its raw probabilities tended to mean something statistical, the tuned model's confidences are shaped by what raters rewarded, and it took a share of blunt honesty ("I don't know") with it. Sycophancy, as noted, is not a bug so much as an equilibrium. And none of this teaching is edit-proof: it's weights, not law. What remains for the corridor is the machine's other frontier — inputs that were never text at all, and the parts of the blueprint that rivals are trying to replace. The last door is open.
