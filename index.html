Transformers from Scratch,
Revisited

counting → dot products → lookups → attention → a modern decoder-only stack

This is a rebuild of Brandon Rohrer's 2021 essay Transformers from Scratch, which he generously released under CC0. The original's great idea — that you can reconstruct a transformer starting from nothing but counting and arithmetic, with the dot product as a search operation at the center of it all — is preserved intact. What's changed: a handful of factual errors are fixed (there's a ledger at the end), the architecture described is the decoder-only stack that powers today's language models, positional encoding gets the full chapter it deserved (including RoPE), and residual connections get reframed as the residual stream — a perspective that quietly flattens the whole tower of layers into something closer to a wide bank of parallel workers. Almost.

The promise is the same as the original's: no prerequisites beyond arithmetic. We'll add each concept one at a time, as it's needed, and by the end there will be no mysterious black boxes left — just a few clearly marked doors — some already open onto pages of their own, the rest bearing signs that say what's behind them.

01One-hot encoding

In the beginning were the words. Our first job is to turn them into numbers so we can do math on them.

Imagine we're building a computer that responds to typed commands, and our whole language is three words: files, find, my. We could just number them — files = 1, find = 2, my = 3 — and represent "find my files" as [2, 3, 1]. That works, but there's a format computers like even better: one-hot encoding. Each word becomes a vector as long as the vocabulary, all zeros except for a single one in that word's slot.

             files  find   my
  files   [    1     0     0  ]
  find    [    0     1     0  ]
  my      [    0     0     1  ]
fig 1 · a one-hot encoded vocabulary. one row per word, one column per slot.

A sentence becomes a stack of these vectors — a two-dimensional array. I'll use "vector" and "one-dimensional array" interchangeably from here on, and likewise "matrix" and "two-dimensional array".

This looks like a wasteful way to store three words, and it is. Its value shows up in the next two sections, where it turns arithmetic into something that behaves like retrieval.

02The dot product is a search operation

To take the dot product of two vectors, multiply their corresponding elements and add up the results. That's the whole definition. It also goes by scarier names — inner product, scalar product — but it's just multiply-and-add.

Here is the observation the entire transformer rests on. The dot product of a one-hot vector with itself is 1. The dot product of a one-hot vector with any different one-hot vector is 0 — the ones never line up.

  match:      [0 1 0] · [0 1 0]  =  0·0 + 1·1 + 0·0  =  1
  no match:   [0 1 0] · [1 0 0]  =  0·1 + 1·0 + 0·0  =  0
fig 2 · the dot product as an exact-match test.

So a dot product against a one-hot vector answers a question: is this the word I'm looking for? It's a search probe. And it generalizes gracefully. If instead of a crisp one-hot vector you have a blend — a vector of weights over several words — the dot product with a one-hot probe reads out how strongly that word is present. The answer stops being yes/no and becomes a relevance score.

Hold onto that framing. Everything that follows — lookup tables, Markov models, and eventually attention itself — is a matter of doing this search at scale, and then making the search terms learnable.

03Matrix multiplication as a table lookup

Matrix multiplication is just dot products in bulk: to multiply matrix A by matrix B, take the dot product of every row of A with every column of B and arrange the results in a grid. The number of columns in A has to match the number of rows in B, and the answer has A's rows and B's columns.

Now combine that with one-hot vectors and something delightful happens. Multiply a one-hot row vector by a matrix B, and the result is exactly one row of B — the row whose index matches the position of the 1.

                    ┌ row 0 ┐
  [0 1 0]    ×      │ row 1 │     =    row 1
                    └ row 2 ┘
fig 3 · a one-hot vector pulls a single row out of a matrix. multiplication acting as retrieval.

Multiplying by a one-hot vector is a table lookup, spelled as arithmetic. Stack several one-hot vectors into a matrix and you fetch several rows at once, in whatever order the ones dictate. This trick — using multiplication to pull particular rows out of a table — is the core of how transformers work. It matters because arithmetic, unlike an if statement or a hash table, is differentiable: we can nudge it, measure how the output changes, and therefore learn it. We've smuggled database retrieval into calculus territory.

04First order sequence model

Back to language. Suppose our command computer only ever hears three sentences:

show me my directories please.
show me my files please.
show me my photos please.

The vocabulary is seven words. A natural way to model the sequences is a transition model: for each word, what's likely to come next? If users ask for photos half the time, files 30% of the time, and directories the rest, then after my the next-word probabilities are 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, and everywhere else the next word is certain. A model where the prediction depends only on the current word is a first order Markov chain.

Markov chains fit beautifully into a matrix: one row per word, one column per word, each cell holding the probability that the column's word follows the row's word. Each row sums to one. And now our lookup trick earns its keep: to ask "what comes after my?", multiply the one-hot vector for my by the transition matrix. Out comes the row for my — a probability distribution over next words. Prediction is retrieval.

              dirs  files   me    my  photos please show
  my       [  .2     .3     0     0    .5      0     0  ]
fig 4 · one row of the transition matrix: the distribution over what follows "my".

05Second order sequence model

Predicting the next word from just the current one is like guessing a tune after hearing a single note. Two notes go a lot further. Consider a model that only ever sees these two sentences, 40/60:

check whether the battery ran down please.
check whether the program ran please.

A first order model hits a wall at ran: sometimes down follows, sometimes please, and it can only shrug and quote the base rates. But a model that looks at the two most recent words knows that battery ran is always followed by down and program ran by please. The branch disappears; uncertainty collapses.

The price shows up in the bookkeeping. A second order transition matrix needs a row for every pair of words: with a vocabulary of N, that's N² rows. The rows become more confident — more ones, fewer fractions — but the table is exploding.

06Second order sequence model with skips

Now the hard case. Two sentences, equally likely:

check the program log and find out whether it ran please.
check the battery log and find out whether it ran down please.

To pick the word after ran, you need to remember a word from eight positions back. An eighth order model would need N⁸ rows — absurd for any real vocabulary. So we cheat, productively. Keep the model second order, but instead of only pairing ran with its immediate predecessor, pair it with every word that came before it, one pair at a time: (check, ran), (the, ran), (battery, ran), and so on. Each pair is a feature, and each feature gets a row of votes over next words. To predict, activate every feature the sentence contains, sum up their vote rows, and take the winner.

This still captures a long range dependency — the (battery, ran) feature votes hard for down, the (program, ran) feature votes hard for please — while throwing away most word-order information about the intervening words. What remains is surprisingly powerful. It's also, note, no longer a Markov model: rows stopped being states and became features; cells stopped being probabilities and became votes.

There's a problem, though. Almost all of those features are useless. Words like check and log appear in both sentences, so their pairs with ran vote 50/50 and just add noise. Summed up, the informative signal from battery or program wins by a sliver — 5 votes to 4. In a real, messy language model, a margin like that would drown in statistical noise.

07Masking, or: selectively ignoring things is attention

The fix is to stop listening to the uninformative features. Build a mask: a vector with ones at the positions of features worth keeping — (battery, ran) and (program, ran) — and zeros everywhere else. Multiply it element-by-element against the vector of active features, and the noise vanishes before the votes are ever summed. The 5-to-4 margin becomes 1-to-0. What was a 25% edge becomes certainty.

This act of selective ignoring — dynamically choosing which parts of the past to consult and which to suppress — is the attention in the title of the 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need. What we've built so far is a conceptual approximation of it: the real mechanism is soft rather than binary, operates in a compressed space rather than on raw words, and is learned rather than hand-picked. We'll close each of those gaps in turn. But the essential move — a search over the past, deciding what's relevant to right now — is already here, and it's made of nothing but the dot-product lookups from sections 02 and 03.

08Rest stop and an off ramp

Congratulations on making it this far — and you're free to stop here. The selective-second-order-with-skips model is an honest first approximation of what generative language models do: build features that pair the current token with relevant earlier context, suppress the irrelevant ones, and let the survivors vote on what comes next. It's not the complete story, but it's the central thrust of it.

Everything from here on closes the gap between that cartoon and the real implementation, and nearly all of it is driven by three engineering realities:

1. Computers are shockingly good at matrix multiplication. An entire hardware industry exists to make it fast. Any computation you can express as matmuls rides the bullet train.

2. Every step must be differentiable. We hand-picked our votes and masks. In practice they're learned by backpropagation, which needs to trace how a small change in any parameter changes the loss — so every operation along the way must have a derivative.

3. The gradient must be smooth and well-conditioned. Training only works if small parameter steps produce small, informative changes in loss — no cliffs, no vast plateaus, no canyon floors. A surprising amount of transformer anatomy (normalization, residual connections, the √d in the attention formula) exists purely to keep the gradient landscape pleasant.

09Attention as matrix multiplication

So far the mask fell out of the sky: we looked at the sentences and picked it by hand. A real model has to find the right mask on its own, for every token, in every context — and to be trainable, it has to do so with differentiable operations. We already own the perfect tool. Stack the mask vectors for every possible word of interest into a table, and use our one-hot-lookup trick to pull out the relevant one.

This lookup is exactly the QKᵀ term inside the attention equation:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dk ) V

The query Q is the probe — the thing we're searching on behalf of. The keys K are the table being searched. The table stores one key per row — but a matrix multiply dots the rows of its left operand against the columns of its right, so K gets transposed (the ᵀ) to turn each key into a column for the queries to score against. Every cell of QKᵀ is a dot product between one query and one key: one search probe scored against one candidate, precisely the match test from section 02. The softmax and the √dk are refinements we'll earn properly in section 16; the values V are what actually gets retrieved once the search decides where to look. For now the point is this: attention is a differentiable lookup table, and the lookup is a batch of dot-product searches.

10The MLP: building features and counting votes

The other thing we hand-waved was the feature table itself. Attention can gather battery and ran into the same place, but something still has to construct the combined feature "battery occurred, and ran is current" and map it to votes. That something is a small two-layer neural network — the block the paper calls the feed-forward network and everyone now calls the MLP.

It works in two steps, and both are our familiar friends. The first matrix multiplication scores the incoming vector against a large bank of learned probes — each row of the first weight matrix is a feature detector, asking its own dot-product question of the input. A nonlinearity then zeroes out the detectors that didn't fire. (The original used ReLU, which simply replaces negatives with zero. Modern models mostly use a smoother gated variant called SwiGLU, where one projection multiplicatively gates another; the reason is empirical — it trains a bit better — and the mental model is unchanged.) The second matrix multiplication then acts as the transition table: each detected feature contributes its learned row of votes over what to write out.

In other words: the first layer asks questions, the second layer casts votes. Detect a pattern, look up what it implies. This reading of the MLP as a bank of key–value memories isn't just a teaching cartoon, either — it's how interpretability researchers have since found real MLP layers to behave, with individual detectors firing on recognizable patterns and writing out predictable contributions. It's also where most of a transformer's parameters live: the MLP's hidden width is typically several times the model width, and across a whole model the MLPs hold roughly two-thirds of the weights. Attention decides where to look; the MLP does most of the remembering. That lopsided ledger is also a lever, and the door that pulls it is open: mixture of experts.

One honest confession, inherited from the original essay. Describing transformers as a "selective second order model with skips" was always a simplification, and here's exactly where it leaks: a learned feature detector is under no obligation to detect a word pair. It can fire on triples, on a word's absence, on soft blends of many things at once. The pairs-and-votes story is a floor on what these layers can express, not a ceiling.

11Sequence completion

A model that predicts the next token becomes a model that writes by the simplest loop imaginable. Feed in a prompt. Run a forward pass. Out comes a probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next position. Choose a token from it, append it to the sequence, and repeat until satisfied (or until you hit a stop token, whichever comes first).

Choosing "the" token deserves a sentence more than it usually gets. Always taking the single most probable token is called greedy decoding, and it's the obvious move, but it tends to produce repetitive, over-safe text. In practice tokens are sampled from the distribution, with a temperature knob that sharpens or flattens it, usually after truncating the long tail of implausible tokens (top-p and friends). The fact that generation is sampling, not lookup, is why the same prompt can yield different answers.

Notice also what the loop implies: to generate token 1,001 we apparently re-process tokens 1 through 1,000 from scratch. We don't, in practice — the keys and values computed for old tokens never change, so they're cached and reused. That single observation reshapes the economics of inference, and its door is now open: the KV cache.

12Embeddings

As described so far, our machinery is catastrophically oversized. With a realistic vocabulary of, say, 100,000 tokens, one-hot vectors are 100,000 elements long, and tables over word pairs need 10 billion rows. Worse, a model built on exact word identity learns nothing transferable: everything it learns about cat tells it nothing about kitten.

The fix is the embedding. Take the N-dimensional one-hot space, where every word sits alone on its own axis, and project all the words down into a much lower-dimensional space — thousands of dimensions rather than a hundred thousand — arranged so that words which behave similarly land near each other. Projection, happily, is just our lookup trick one more time: multiplying a one-hot vector by an [N × d] embedding matrix pulls out that word's row, which now holds its d coordinates rather than a probability row. Every word becomes a dense little bundle of coordinates, and whatever the model learns about one point quietly applies to its neighbors. That's the real prize: generalization. Each training example teaches a whole neighborhood.

The embedding matrix is learned along with everything else, and the number of dimensions is a dial trading capacity for cost. From here on, everything happens to these dense vectors; the one-hot representation appears only at the very entrance and exit of the model. (That's a bigger deal than it looks: anything else that can be turned into dense vectors — image patches, audio frames — can enter by the same gate, and that door is open: beyond text.) And note what this does to our search framing: dot-product matching in embedding space is no longer an exact identity test but a semantic one. The probe that matches battery now also half-matches charger. The search got fuzzy, on purpose.

13Position: from wiggles to rotations

Here's an awkward fact about everything we've built since introducing attention: it's a bag. The attention sums and the MLP's feature detectors treat the context as an unordered collection — shuffle the earlier words and, absent any other signal, nothing changes. But "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" deserve different continuations. Position has to be injected somewhere. This chapter was a stub in the original essay, by its author's own admission. The field has since more or less settled the question, so let's do it properly.

Absolute positions: add a position vector

The first family of solutions marks each token with where it is. Learn (or design) one vector per position — a vector for "slot 1", a vector for "slot 2" — and add it to the token's embedding on the way in. GPT-2 learned these vectors from data. The original 2017 paper designed them instead, as sinusoids: each pair of embedding dimensions traces a circle as you walk along the sequence, with different pairs cycling at different frequencies, fast ones distinguishing neighbors and slow ones distinguishing distant regions. Like reading a clock: the second hand separates nearby instants, the hour hand separates afternoons. Rohrer described this as a "circular wiggle," which remains the best name for it.

Absolute schemes work, but they answer a slightly wrong question. For language, what mostly matters is not "this token is at position 1,847" but "this token is three tokens before that one." Relationships are relative. And baking absolute slot labels into the embeddings means position information sloshes around through every downstream computation, whether relevant or not. That sloshing is measurable, not rhetorical: added-in position and content compete for the same coordinates of the shared vector, and when one small-scale study gave the sinusoids a learned linear layer of their own, training used it to rotate the positional basis nearly out of the content subspace — a ~7× drop in overlap (Lehtinen, 2026), echoing the "untied" positional designs tried in language models. Even the additive scheme wants position and content disentangled; it just lacks the freedom. The next idea grants that wish structurally.

Relative position: rotate the search probes — RoPE

The modern default, Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), is a sharper idea, and it slots directly into our search framing. Don't touch the embeddings, the values, or the residual stream at all. Instead, at the moment of the attention search, rotate the probes. Take each query and key vector, treat its dimensions two at a time as points on a plane, and rotate each pair by an angle proportional to the token's position — again with a spectrum of frequencies across the pairs, fast hands and slow hands.

fig 5 · one two-dimensional slice of a query or key under RoPE. the vector's content sets the hand's length and starting angle; its position winds it forward.

Why is that clever? Because rotation doesn't change lengths, and the dot product between two vectors depends only on their lengths and the angle between them. Rotate a query forward by its position m and a key forward by its position n, and the rotations partially cancel: the match score depends only on the offset m − n. The search automatically asks "how well does this key match, given how far back it sits?" — relative position, obtained for free, exactly where position matters (the matching) and nowhere it doesn't (the content being retrieved). Attention scores can now learn distance-sensitive behaviors — "match this pattern, but prefer recent instances" — without any position information contaminating the token representations themselves.

Honorable mentions in the same spirit: ALiBi skips the trigonometry and simply subtracts a distance-proportional penalty from every attention score, a blunt "prefer nearby" prior that extrapolates gracefully; and some recent models use no positional scheme in certain layers at all, since the causal mask alone leaks enough ordering information for the model to reconstruct position (which quietly amends this chapter's opening "bag" claim — the mask was already breaking perfect shuffle-blindness). The choice of scheme also turns out to be the crux of extending models to very long contexts — that door is open: long context.

14Unembedding and softmax

After the model has done its thinking, the final vector at the last position lives in embedding space, and we need a score for every token in the vocabulary. Getting back out is one more projection — an unembedding matrix, shaped [d × N], the mirror image of the way in. (Some models literally reuse the embedding matrix, transposed, for this — "tied embeddings" — which is tidy, saves parameters, and makes the symmetry explicit: entering vocabulary space and leaving it are the same lookup, run in opposite directions.)

The result is a dense vector of raw scores — logits — with high values for plausible tokens, middling values for their neighbors in embedding space, negatives for the implausible. To turn scores into a probability distribution we use softmax: exponentiate every score and divide by the sum of the exponentials. Softmax earns its place three times over. It produces genuine probabilities, so candidates can be compared and sampled. It behaves like a "soft argmax" — a clear winner is amplified toward 1, but near-ties are preserved as near-ties rather than crushed, which is what makes non-greedy sampling meaningful. And it is differentiable, so the whole assembly can be trained end to end. This same function, we're about to see, also runs the attention search — it is the transformer's utility infielder.

15Multi-head attention

Time to face the shapes squarely. A short cast of numbers, with modern values for scale: vocabulary size N (~32k–256k today), sequence length n (thousands to millions), and the model width dmodel (512 in the 2017 paper; 4,096 in Llama-2-7B; five figures in frontier models). The token sequence enters as an [n × dmodel] matrix and — this is the quiet organizing principle of the whole architecture — every block reads and writes that same shape, all the way up.

Within an attention block, learned matrices WQ, WK, WV project the stream into queries, keys, and values. And here we correct the record on a point the original essay got backwards. It's tempting to say we need multiple heads because softmax focuses attention on a single element. But softmax does no such thing by necessity — it can happily spread weight across many positions (the √dk scaling exists partly to keep it from saturating into a hard argmax). The real motivation is different: one search at a time is the limitation. A single head applies one learned notion of relevance. But resolving a pronoun, tracking an open quotation mark, and copying a variable name are different searches with different keys, wanted simultaneously.

So the block runs h searches in parallel — the heads — each with its own WQ, WK, WV, each projecting into its own small subspace of dimension dk = dmodel/h (64 in the paper, and, by remarkable inertia, usually still ~64–128 today). Each head zooms in on the slice of embedding space relevant to its own question. Running h small searches costs about the same as one big one — the projections slice the width rather than multiply it — and the h results are concatenated and passed through one final output projection, WO, to write back to the stream.

One modern wrinkle worth naming: in most current models the heads no longer get private keys and values. Groups of query heads share a single K/V pair — grouped-query attention (GQA), or in the extreme, one K/V pair for all heads (MQA). Quality barely notices; the motivation is the inference-time cache from section 11, whose size scales with the number of K/V heads. Many different questions, asked against a shared index.

16Single head attention, revisited

Now we can read the full equation with everything it earned:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dk + M ) V

QKᵀ is [n × n]: every position's query scored, by dot product, against every position's key. Row i is position i's search over the entire sequence. Dividing by √dk keeps these scores in a civilized range — dot products of high-dimensional random vectors grow with √dimension, and unscaled they'd push softmax into saturated, nearly-gradient-free territory, violating engineering reality #3.

M is the causal mask, and here we fix the original essay's mechanics. For next-token prediction, position i must not consult positions after i — that would be reading the answer key. The mask is not a multiplication by zeros and ones. It's an [n × n] matrix added to the scores: 0 where looking is allowed (the lower triangle), −∞ where it isn't. After softmax, e−∞ = 0, so forbidden positions get exactly zero weight and the remaining weights renormalize over the allowed ones — which a post-hoc multiplicative mask wouldn't do. Zeroing scores wouldn't even hide anything, since a score of 0 still claims positive probability after softmax. Add negative infinity before the softmax; let e^x do the censoring.

The softmaxed rows then weight the value vectors: each position's output is a blend of the values at the positions its search matched. Retrieval, completed. Note also that the whole [n × n] table is computed at once — every position runs its search in the same pair of matmuls, which is precisely the parallelism that made transformers trainable at scale where recurrent models were stuck marching token by token. Attention is quadratic in sequence length, though. One ingenious way of paying that bill — computing the same table without ever writing it down — has an open door: FlashAttention. Ways of softening the quadratic itself are signposted in section 22.

Two final honesty notes, both preserved from the original. First: the searches are conducted in learned subspaces, so attention relates kinds of content, not individual words — the semantic fuzziness from section 12 applies to every probe. Second: what attention passes along is position-to-position. It says "blend in what's at position 7," whatever position 7 turned out to contain. The routing and the content are cleanly separated, which is much of why the mechanism is interpretable at all.

17The residual stream: the tower that's almost a field

Here is the piece of plumbing that changes how the whole building looks. Around every attention block and every MLP runs a residual connection (skip connection): the block's input is added back to its output. No block ever replaces the signal; every block computes a correction and adds it.

The traditional justifications are real and worth keeping. Residual connections keep the gradient landscape smooth — even if a block learns to pass nothing, the identity path guarantees that changes upstream still reach downstream, so training doesn't strand itself on plateaus. And they guarantee the model can never entirely forget its input: attention is a filter, and a filter that's working well blocks most things; the residual path carries the original signal past it, unconditionally.

But take the additions seriously and a stronger picture emerges. That [n × dmodel] matrix flowing up the page — the green line running down this one — is a shared communication channel: the residual stream. It begins as embeddings plus whatever position scheme touches it, and ends at the unembedding. Every component along the way does exactly one thing: it reads from the stream through its input projections, computes something, and writes its result back by addition. Nothing else. The stream is the only memory, the only bus, the only way any part of the model talks to any other part.

fig 6 · the residual stream as a bus. every head and MLP is a device that reads the stream and adds a contribution back.

An objection deserves an answer before we go further: if hundreds of components all add their outputs into the same vector, why don't they trample each other into mush? Because the stream is wide, and wide spaces are roomy. A dmodel-dimensional vector has room for dmodel perfectly orthogonal directions — and far more nearly orthogonal ones. A component that writes along its own direction is simply invisible to a reader probing along an orthogonal one: their dot product is zero, which in the terms of section 02 means "not what I'm searching for." Addition works as a bus because writes can be steered into subspaces that other readers ignore — this is how h heads' contributions coexist in one write, and how an early head's note survives untouched until a later head comes searching for it.

The remarkable part is that nobody has to arrange this — and here we can lean on a measurement. A recent audit of input encoders (Lehtinen, 2026) set up the smallest possible version of the problem: C signal channels, each with its own learned linear projection, all summed into one stream vector per time step. Many writers, one bus, no coordination imposed. Now, high-dimensional geometry hands such writers near-orthogonality for free — random directions in 64 dimensions overlap by only ~0.1 in cosine terms — so finding the trained projections near-orthogonal would by itself prove nothing, and the audit is careful to measure against each run's own random starting point. The result: training actively tightened the projections well beyond the free default, cutting their mutual overlap ~2.6×, in every seed, with no regularizer asking for it. Bolting on an explicit orthogonality penalty made the geometry another 4× cleaner and improved the loss not at all — the constraint that mattered was already being discovered. Two more of this section's claims got caught on camera in the same experiment. The bus allocates bandwidth by importance: channels that actually drive the outcome earned roughly twice the write norm of distractor channels, with no gating mechanism anywhere in the architecture. And the writes persist through depth: linear probes could still read each channel's identity out of the stream after every one of the model's blocks — which the paper attributes, citing the same residual-stream framework as this section, to precisely the mechanics we've assembled: sublayers write by addition, and (pre-)normalization only rescales what they read, so what's written at the bottom stays readable at the top. It's an input stem rather than a stack of heads, so carry the scale of the numbers with care — but the protocol gradient descent invents there is the one this whole section describes: orthogonal writes, dot-product reads, discovered rather than designed.

One caveat keeps the picture honest. That audit runs in the roomy regime — far fewer writers than dimensions, so everyone can have their own direction. A language model does not: it wants to represent vastly more features than the stream has dimensions, and pressed for capacity, training overpacks the bus — squeezing in more nearly-orthogonal directions than the space strictly holds, tolerating faint crosstalk as rent. That phenomenon, superposition, is much of why single dimensions of the stream mean nothing in isolation, and why reading a model's mind is a research field rather than an afternoon.

Squint at that diagram and the "deep stack of layers" starts to dissolve. If every component just reads the shared stream and adds to it, then a 32-layer model is less a tower of transformations than a wide bank of hundreds of heads and MLPs, all wired to one bus, operating nearly side by side. Layer boundaries stop looking architectural and start looking like a scheduling detail — an ordering imposed on a crowd of otherwise parallel workers. Rohrer's conveyor-belt intuition — each station takes what it can use and adds its work back onto the line, no station obligated to catch everything — was reaching for exactly this, and the residual stream is that intuition made precise.

Almost. The ordering is a scheduling detail with one crucial consequence: a component can read what earlier components wrote. That's the loophole through which depth stays meaningful — sequential composition. The classic example is the induction pattern, the mechanism behind a transformer's knack for repeating sequences it has seen before. One head, in an early layer, writes onto each token's stream position a note about which token preceded it. A head in a later layer then searches those notes: holding the current token as its query, it matches positions whose note says "I was preceded by that token," and retrieves what came next last time. Neither head can do this alone; the second head's search key only exists because the first head wrote it. Two dot-product lookups, composed through the stream, yield an algorithm — copy what followed last time — that no single lookup can express.

So the honest summary is: a transformer is a broad, almost-flat bank of parallel workers sharing one additive channel, with just enough sequencing to let workers build on each other's notes. Most components act in parallel and independently; a few chains of composition carry the clever algorithms. That "almost" is where much of the magic lives, and it's the working picture of the interpretability researchers who take these models apart for a living.

18Normalization

Neural networks are nonlinear, which makes them expressive but touchy about the scale of their signals — and a stream that only ever gets added to has a natural tendency to grow. Layer normalization is the thermostat: rescale a vector so its values have a standard deviation of one (classically also shifting the mean to zero), then let a couple of learned parameters set the preferred scale. Applied at every block, it keeps signals in the range where gradients behave, at every depth.

Two updates from the modern world, one small and one that quietly mattered a great deal. The small one: most current models use RMSNorm, which skips the mean-subtraction and just divides by the root-mean-square — cheaper, and empirically nothing of value was lost. The big one is placement. The 2017 design normalized after each block, on the residual path itself ("post-norm") — meaning the normalization repeatedly re-scaled the stream, interrupting the clean identity highway. Post-norm transformers were notoriously delicate to train, needing careful learning-rate warmup, and got worse with depth. The fix, standard since GPT-2, is pre-norm: normalize each block's input, on the branch, and leave the stream itself untouched from embedding to unembedding. The residual bus of section 17 runs clean end to end — each device normalizes what it reads, and writes back to an unmolested channel. Deep pre-norm transformers train stably almost by default, and it's fair to say this unglamorous reordering is one of the changes that made very large models practical. Nobody entirely knows why normalization helps as much as it does; that admission from the original essay has aged perfectly.

19Layers, revisited

We can now say briskly what a modern transformer block is, because every part has been built. One block = attention (read the stream, search the past, write back) followed by an MLP (read the stream, detect features, write back votes), each wrapped in pre-norm and a residual connection. A model is that block repeated L times — 6 in the 2017 paper, 32 in Llama-2-7B, around a hundred in the largest current models — between an embedding at the bottom and an unembedding at the top.

Why repeat it? The original essay's answer — redundancy and trainability — survives, in refined form. With randomly initialized weights, a model with only one narrow path to competence will likely never find it; hundreds of near-parallel workers on a shared stream offer countless paths, and gradient descent only has to find some of them. No single head or MLP is load-bearing (models degrade remarkably gracefully when individual components are damaged), which is exactly what the almost-flat picture predicts. And the depth that remains buys composition: enough sequencing for workers to build multi-step algorithms out of each other's notes, as in section 17. Depth in a transformer is less "higher and higher abstraction at every floor" and more "enough rounds of communication for the crowd to coordinate."

20Decoder-only by default

The 2017 transformer was two towers: an encoder that read the source sentence with unmasked, look-anywhere attention, and a decoder that generated the target sentence with causal attention, consulting the encoder through a third attention variant — cross-attention, where the queries come from the decoder but the keys and values come from the encoder's output. Same equation, different guest list: search someone else's sequence instead of your own. It's the right shape for translation, where a fully-visible input is transformed into a generated output, and encoder–decoder models (T5 and kin) still serve well there.

But everything we built in this essay is just the decoder tower, standing alone, and that's no simplification — it's the architecture of essentially every headline language model since GPT-2: the GPT family, Llama, Claude, Gemini, Mistral, and the rest. Causal self-attention over one ever-growing sequence turns out to be enough: the "input" is simply the part of the sequence you were given, and the "output" is the part you generate, with no separate tower needed. If you can frame a task as sequence continuation — and it's remarkable how much can be framed that way — the decoder alone does it.

Time to correct the record on the surrounding taxonomy, because the original essay garbled it. BERT is not a decoder — it's the encoder tower standing alone: bidirectional, unmasked attention, trained to fill in blanks rather than continue text. That makes it a model that reads — producing representations for classification, retrieval, and search — not one that generates. ELMo isn't a transformer at all; it was a bidirectional LSTM, a lovely late flowering of the recurrent tradition that the 2017 paper's title ("attention is all you need — recurrence can go") was aimed squarely against. The family portrait, correctly labeled: encoder-only reads (BERT and its descendants, most embedding models), decoder-only writes (nearly everything you've chatted with), encoder–decoder transforms (translation, and tasks shaped like it). As for how a raw sequence-continuer becomes something you'd want to chat with — that's post-training, and its door is open: after pretraining.

21Tokenizing and byte pair encoding

One last piece of honesty about the ground floor. We've been saying "words" throughout, and models don't use words. A dictionary of whole words is a losing game — inflections, typos, names, code, other languages, emoji — and individual characters lose too: they carry almost no meaning for the embedding space to organize, and stretch every text into an enormously long sequence for a mechanism whose cost grows quadratically with length.

The standing compromise is byte pair encoding, in its modern byte-level form. Start from raw bytes — not characters, bytes, so that every possible string is representable and no input is ever truly out-of-vocabulary. Scan a large corpus; find the most frequent adjacent pair of units; merge it into a new unit; repeat until the vocabulary reaches a chosen size. Frequent words end up as single tokens, rare words as a few chunks, and gibberish decomposes all the way to bytes. Vocabulary sizes have drifted upward with the models — GPT-2 used ~50k tokens; current models commonly run 100k–256k, largely to tokenize the world's languages and code more evenly.

The vocabulary, in other words, is itself learned from data — inferred, not decreed, which is very much in the spirit of everything above it. Tokenization remains the transformer's least glamorous layer and the source of its most charming failure modes: a model that reasons about black holes but stumbles counting the r's in strawberry is a model that has genuinely never seen the letters — only the tokens.

22The doors

The core transformer is now fully assembled, and this essay stops at its edges on purpose. Everything below is real and important, but none of it changes the machine you just built — it changes how fast the machine runs, how long its memory is, and what happens to it after pretraining. So each topic gets a door. An open door leads to its own page, written in the same spirit as this one; a marked door carries a sign saying what's behind it, until its page is written too. The corridor grows one door at a time.

door 01 · open

The KV cache, and the economics of inference

Generation re-reads the whole past for every new token — unless you cache each token's keys and values, which never change. The cache turns generation from quadratic re-work into a fast append, and its memory footprint (which GQA exists to shrink) is the central budget of serving LLMs.

door 02 · open

FlashAttention: the same math, faster

Attention's real cost on modern GPUs isn't arithmetic, it's moving the [n × n] score matrix in and out of memory. FlashAttention computes the identical result in tiles that never leave fast on-chip memory — an exact algorithm, not an approximation, and a lesson in how hardware shapes what "efficient" means.

door 03 · open

Long context

How does a model trained on 8k tokens read a novel? Mostly by careful manipulation of RoPE's rotation frequencies (position interpolation, YaRN, and kin), plus attention variants — sliding windows, sparse patterns — that soften the quadratic wall. Section 13 is the prerequisite.

door 04 · open

Mixture of experts

Since the MLPs hold most of the parameters, replace each one with a panel of expert MLPs and a tiny router that sends each token to a couple of them. Total parameters balloon; compute per token doesn't. Most frontier models are now sparse in this sense.

door 05 · open

After pretraining: instruction tuning and RLHF

Everything in this essay produces a next-token predictor. The step from "continues text" to "answers questions and follows instructions" is post-training — supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, then optimization against learned preferences. Same architecture, different objective, very different behavior.

door 06 · open

Beyond text, and beyond attention

Images, audio, and other modalities enter the same stream as sequences of dense vectors — the trick anticipated in the original essay's audio section. Meanwhile state-space models and hybrids ask how much of attention's job recurrence can take back. The residual-stream picture survives all of it.
